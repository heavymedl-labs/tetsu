# Passkey domains research

Research for decision 3 of [#9](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/9), run 2026-11-11 by Kurt Medley
with a coding agent: where Tetsu hosts login and which domain its passkeys belong to. It builds on the
[identity research](identity.md). Four research passes covered the WebAuthn rules, platforms with custom domains,
hostname layout, and migration and what customers see; their notes, with every source, are in
[passkey-domains/](passkey-domains/). This is research, not a decision. Where it disagrees with an ADR, the ADR
wins. Facts and prices are as read on 2026-11-11.

## Bind Tetsu's passkeys before anyone enrolls

The evidence favours **option A: one relying party ID (RP ID) on a registrable domain Tetsu owns, with all login on Tetsu's hosts, carrying business branding, and a one-time-code handoff to any business custom domain**. Option B (login on each business's domain) gives the best branding but strands a business's customer passkeys whenever its domain changes. Option C (Related Origin Requests) is capped at five domain labels in practice, so it cannot serve tenants. The first and most urgent finding is about the demo. **`onrender.com` is on the Public Suffix List, so a passkey made on `tetsu-demo.onrender.com` can only ever use that exact host as its RP ID.** No mechanism moves it to a future Tetsu domain, so every real person who enrolls before Tetsu owns a domain will have to enroll again. The RP ID is the one identity choice that cannot be changed later. X's forced passkey re-enrollment in 2025 shows the cost: users were caught in login loops and some were locked out. The choice also decides what customers see. Passkey prompts and managers show the RP ID and the username, not the business name. Under option A, Lee Park's two separate accounts at Anvil Row and Juniper Lane would appear as two entries for the same Tetsu domain unless Tetsu plans for it. This report sets out options and trade-offs for decision 3 of the ADR on [#9](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/9), the customer login host. It does not make the decision. The weakest evidence covers Firefox's support for Related Origin Requests, Safari's behaviour in frames, and how password managers label several accounts under one domain.

## The demo host can never be Tetsu's passkey home

An RP ID is a bare domain with no scheme or port. It must equal the page's effective domain or be a "registrable domain suffix" of it, and a credential "can only be used for authentication with the same entity (as identified by RP ID) it was registered with" ([WebAuthn L3](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#rp-id)). WebAuthn Level 3 became a W3C Recommendation on 25 August 2026 with no substantive changes since May ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/REC-webauthn-3-20260825/)). The suffix test rests on the Public Suffix List, and **Render submitted `onrender.com` to the list's private section** ([PSL](https://publicsuffix.org/list/public_suffix_list.dat)). So `tetsu-demo.onrender.com` is a registrable domain in its own right, and its only valid RP ID is itself. `onrender.com` is rejected as an RP ID, just as `com` is. Paths play no part, so every business at `/<slug>` on the demo already shares one RP ID.

That has three consequences for enrollment timing. First, any passkey created on the demo is tied to a Render-owned hostname forever. Second, the only way to keep such passkeys working after a move is Related Origin Requests in reverse. The demo host would serve `/.well-known/webauthn` listing the new Tetsu origin, and Tetsu's own pages would keep calling WebAuthn with `rpId: "tetsu-demo.onrender.com"` ([WebAuthn L3 §5.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#sctn-related-origins)). Tetsu would then depend on a Render service name for as long as those passkeys live, which sits badly with ADR-0003's rule against provider-specific services, and browsers without support would fail. Third, putting a Tetsu domain on the PSL is not a workaround. The list's maintainers say test and beta projects, or projects with fewer than thousands of users, are "quite likely to be declined", and browsers pick up changes on their own schedules ([PSL guidelines](https://github.com/publicsuffix/list/wiki/Guidelines)).

The cheap path is to **register Tetsu's domain and set the final RP ID before anyone outside the maintainer enrolls a passkey**, which in practice means before login in [#20](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/20) reaches anyone outside the maintainer. If #20 ships on the demo first, its passkeys should be treated as disposable, and the pages should say they will be reset. Demo accounts still need multi-factor login, because Microsoft's Midnight Blizzard breach began at a test tenant with no MFA ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/microsoft-reveals-how-hackers-breached-its-exchange-online-accounts/amp/)). Render supports custom and wildcard domains with automatic Let's Encrypt certificates and charges $0.25 a month for each domain beyond the plan's allowance (2 on Hobby, 15 on Pro). A wildcard needs the apex on Render too ([Render](https://render.com/docs/custom-domains)). The domain itself is therefore the main cost of acting early.

## Three options, and only one scales to thousands of unrelated domains

Real platforms split two ways. Platforms with a consumer identity across merchants, such as Shopify's Shop, Substack, and Mindbody, log people in on the platform's domain. Site builders such as Ghost, Wix, and Kajabi keep members per site, on the site's domain. Shopify is the closest match to Tetsu and has both halves. Customer account pages started at `shopify.com/<shop id>/account`, then moved to a branded `account.<store domain>`, and the login page moved last, in May 2025 ([Shopify Help](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/domains/add-a-domain/connecting-domains/connect-domain-customer-account); [Shopify Changelog](https://changelog.shopify.com/posts/branded-urls-on-customer-login-pages)). Passkeys never moved. A Shop passkey belongs to the platform-wide Shop identity and covers shop.app, Shop Pay checkout, and "logging into customer accounts" ([Shop Help](https://help.shop.app/en/shop/shop-pay-settings/security/passkeys)). Shopify does not publish its RP ID. User reports show `accounts.shopify.com` ([Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/cannot-login-using-passkeys-on-firefox-using-bitwarden/659008)), so treat that as unconfirmed. The lesson is that **the branded login page and the passkey binding are separate decisions**, and the largest platform in the notes binds passkeys to its own domain.

Identity vendors tell the same story. Auth0 gives "each custom domain... its own RP ID", says "a passkey enrolled on `login.brand1.com` cannot be used on `login.brand2.com`", and says passkeys "cannot be transferred" ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/docs/customize/custom-domains/multiple-custom-domains/passkeys)). WorkOS says to set the custom domain before enabling passkeys, because adding it later "would prevent the usage of passkeys that were registered on the old domain" ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/docs/authkit/passkeys)). Clerk advises against passkeys on its satellite domains ([Clerk](https://clerk.com/docs/guides/dashboard/dns-domains/satellite-domains)). Okta calls central login "same-party federation", "the most robust and platform-agnostic option" when domains share no suffix ([Okta Support](https://support.okta.com/help/s/article/passkey-implementation-and-architecture-for-multiple-domains)). No vendor offers one passkey that works across a platform's many unrelated tenant domains.

| | A. Central login, Tetsu RP ID, handoff | B. Login on each business's domain | C. One RP ID shared by Related Origin Requests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RP ID | Tetsu's apex, e.g. `tetsu.example` | Each custom domain; Tetsu's host for the rest | Tetsu's apex, used from listed origins |
| Scales to many custom domains | Yes | Yes | **No: about 5 labels** |
| Passkeys survive a business changing or adding a domain | **Yes** | No: its customers re-enroll | Only while it stays within the label budget |
| What the passkey prompt names | Tetsu's domain | The business's domain | Both, e.g. "Sign in to B with your passkey for A?" |
| Address bar during login | Tetsu's host, branded page | Business's domain | Business's domain |
| Precedent | Shop passkeys, Okta, Clerk satellites | Auth0 multiple domains, Ghost, Kajabi | ccTLD and brand variants of one company |
| Main cost | Handoff code and customer-domain branding work | Per-domain WebAuthn origin checks; re-enrollment on every domain change | Browser support gaps; caps out early |

Option A's mechanics are well understood. The custom domain sets a `__Host-` pre-login cookie holding a random state and a PKCE verifier, then redirects to Tetsu's login host. The login host checks the return address against the business's registered domain by exact match, as ASVS 5.0.0 3.7.2 (L2) requires for redirects to another host ([ASVS V3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OWASP/ASVS/v5.0.0/5.0/en/0x12-V3-Web-Frontend-Security.md)). After the passkey ceremony it issues a short-lived, single-use code bound to the user, business, and return origin. RFC 9700 requires codes to be invalidated after first use, requires exact redirect URI matching, and forbids open redirectors ([RFC 9700](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9700.html)). The custom domain redeems the code and sets its own fresh `__Host-` session. Tetsu is one service, so redemption can be a database lookup with an atomic delete rather than a network call. That inference is from the notes, not from a named platform's write-up. Firebase's warning that cross-domain login relying on third-party storage breaks in modern browsers is the reason the custom domain must end with its own first-party cookie ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/auth/web/redirect-best-practices)). Social login fits the same shape, because the IdP callback lands on Tetsu's host.

Option B is real precedent, not a strawman. It fits the per-business customer accounts that #9 already favours, and the passkey prompt names the business's own domain. Its cost is the force ADR-0031 recorded on #9: accounts "must survive a business later adding its own domain". Under B, a business that moves from Tetsu's address to its own domain, changes domain, or loses one strands every customer passkey. Recovery, likely email, then carries that business's whole customer base at once. B also stretches Tetsu's WebAuthn origin validation and DNS state into every tenant's configuration. A variant would set the RP ID to the business's registrable domain (`salon.com` for `book.salon.com`) so its subdomains could change. That would make every host under `salon.com`, including a site builder Tetsu does not control, a valid origin for asking for those passkeys. This inference comes from the suffix rule, and no source tests it.

Option C fails on numbers. The spec requires clients to "support at least five registrable origin labels" and lets client policy set a cap ([WebAuthn L3 §5.11](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#sctn-related-origins)). Chrome's cap is exactly 5, and origins beyond it are ignored ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/webauthn-related-origin-requests)). passkeys.dev says to treat 5 as the maximum and calls the feature a tool for when "federation is not possible" ([passkeys.dev](https://passkeys.dev/docs/advanced/related-origins/)). Five tenants with custom domains would use up the budget, and the sixth would fail quietly. Support also conflicts. passkeys.dev lists Chrome and Edge 128+, Safari 18+, and Firefox 152+ ([passkeys.dev](https://passkeys.dev/device-support/)). web.dev said in January 2026 that Firefox was "still considering" it, and a Mozilla support thread reports failure on Firefox 140 ([support.mozilla.org](https://support.mozilla.org/de/questions/1600732)). No primary Mozilla source confirms it shipped. Related Origin Requests remain useful for a handful of Tetsu's own brand domains, not for tenants.

## Customers will see Tetsu's domain, not the business, in every prompt

Passkey providers label an entry by the RP ID and `user.name`. An Apple engineer says `name` "is meant to be the equivalent of... a username", and "because the `displayName` is never used in the UI, it's also not an option in the API for passkeys" ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/762984)). A vendor reports that `rp.name` does not appear in current browser popups either ([Turnkey](https://docs.turnkey.com/authentication/passkeys/options.md)). That is weaker evidence, and no recent screenshot study across Chrome, Safari, and Windows was found. Under option A, a customer who booked at Juniper Lane sees roughly "tetsu.example / <lee@example.com>" in iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager. Tetsu's own pages can carry the business's brand before and after the system dialog, which is what FIDO's guidance on consistent "handshake" messages around the dialog describes ([FIDO design principles](https://fidoalliance.org/design-guidelines/principles-2/)). The fetched FIDO page was a translation, so its wording is paraphrased.

The sharper problem is the per-business account model. #9's reference case says Lee Park must get two separate accounts, one owned by each business. Under one Tetsu RP ID, those become two passkeys with the same domain and probably the same username. Okta advises against a vendor-owned RP ID for exactly this reason, "to prevent credentials from different organizations from overlapping in the passkey manager" ([Okta Help](https://help.okta.com/oie/en-us/content/topics/identity-engine/authenticators/passkeys-relying-party-id.htm)). Duende notes that when tenants share an RP ID, a passkey for one tenant can be offered at another tenant's origin under that RP ID ([Duende](https://duendesoftware.com/blog/20251014-deep-dive-into-relying-party-id-and-origin-with-passkeys)). The server still decides which account a credential unlocks, so this is confusion, not a breach, as long as every assertion is checked against the business in the session binding. Three designs answer it. The first puts the business name in `user.name`, for example "<lee@example.com> (Juniper Lane)". It shows up in prompts, but it misuses the username field and needs `signalCurrentUserDetails` to keep it current ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/identity/webauthn-signal-api)). The second filters `allowCredentials` to the business's credentials after an email step, which gives up some passkey autofill. The third is Shopify's split: one Tetsu-held credential that proves the person, linked to separate per-business accounts that each business owns. That keeps one entry per person, but it moves part of identity to Tetsu, close to the Mindbody universal-account pattern #9 wants to avoid. How each manager orders or merges duplicate entries was not verified. From prior knowledge only, managers keep separate entries when `user.id` differs.

There is also a fourth layout, A2, that gives per-business labels without option B's fragility. Each business gets a Tetsu-owned subdomain such as `anvil-row.tetsu.example`, used as that business's RP ID, and login always happens there with a handoff to any custom domain. Prompts then name the business's subdomain, and passkeys survive a custom domain change. They do not survive a slug rename. The layout also brings the subdomain costs described in the next section. No platform in the notes uses this exact layout, so it is an untested inference.

Consumer adoption sets the stakes. FIDO's 2026 index reports **36% of logins by passkey, 93% login success versus 78% for traditional MFA**, and logins nearly three times faster ([FIDO Passkey Index 2026](https://fidoalliance.org/passkey-index-2026/)). Those figures come from large, frequent-use services, so they say little about someone booking a haircut twice a year. Placement matters more than domain. Amedia got 48% of new users to create a passkey by prompting right after verification, double its choice-based flow ([Chrome Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/amedia-passkey-webotp-case-study)). On trust, a CHI 2011 study found many users judge pages by "brand, content and previous experiences" rather than the address bar ([Lin et al.](https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2011-DomainHighlighting.CHI.pdf)). No study compares conversion on a platform-domain login with a business-domain one. Shopify's merchants did complain about seeing shopify.com in the address bar ([Shopify Community](https://community.shopify.com/t/main-domain-shows-as-shopify-com-on-the-address-bar/300772)). That is evidence of merchant preference, not of customer drop-off. One phishing argument favours A: a single login domain teaches customers one origin to trust, which still matters for the phishable email-code fallback even though passkeys themselves refuse to answer a lookalike site.

## Hostnames decide realm boundaries, and operators belong apart

The RP ID is also a security boundary. Every origin under the RP ID may ask for its passkeys, so an apex RP ID makes every subdomain part of the trusted set. This follows from the suffix rule ([WebAuthn L3](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#rp-id)), though no source states it in those terms. That matters when choosing a layout for the three realms. ASVS 5.0.0 3.5.4 (L2) wants "separate applications... hosted on different hostnames", 3.3.3 (L2) wants `__Host-` cookies, and 3.4.1 at L2 requires HSTS to cover all subdomains ([ASVS V3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OWASP/ASVS/v5.0.0/5.0/en/0x12-V3-Web-Frontend-Security.md)). None requires separate registrable domains.

| Layout | Example | Passkey effect | Cookie and isolation effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paths on one host | `tetsu.example/<slug>`, `/app`, `/ops` | One RP ID for every realm and business | `__Host-` works as now; arguably fails 3.5.4 if realms count as separate applications |
| Realm subdomains | `tetsu.example/<slug>`, `app.tetsu.example`, `ops.tetsu.example` | Apex RP ID shared, or a host-only RP ID per realm | Same-site, so forgery defence comes from tokens and `Sec-Fetch-*`, never `SameSite` |
| Business subdomains | `anvil-row.tetsu.example` | Apex RP ID shares passkeys across businesses; host RP ID gives A2 | Wildcard on Render; cookie tossing between siblings |
| Separate domain for tenant pages | `tetsusites.example` plus `tetsu.example` | Tenant pages cannot use the app's RP ID without Related Origin Requests, so login redirects | Cross-site isolation without the PSL |
| Business custom domain | `book.salon.com` | Never Tetsu's RP ID; handoff under A | Own `__Host-` cookie per domain |

Subdomains cost something. A subdomain can plant cookies for its parent, and `SameSite` gives no protection because the attack is same-site. GitPod's CVE-2024-21583 used exactly this to bind a victim's Bitbucket to an attacker's account, and it was fixed by adopting `__Host-` ([Snyk Labs](https://labs.snyk.io/resources/hijacking-oauth-flows-via-cookie-tossing/)). `__Host-` is not airtight either. PortSwigger showed in 2025 that a leading Unicode whitespace or a legacy `$Version` parse can forge prefixed cookies on servers that trim names ([PortSwigger](https://portswigger.net/research/cookie-chaos-how-to-bypass-host-and-secure-cookie-prefixes)). How the Rust cookie crates handle these cases was not checked. GitHub moved Pages to `github.io` in 2013 because user script on github.com subdomains could write github.com cookies ([GitHub](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/new-github-pages-domain-github-io/)). Tetsu serves no tenant-authored script, so the path layout's shared origin is acceptable today. That changes if businesses ever add their own HTML.

Operators are the highest-value accounts, and every support-tool incident in the earlier research was an attack on operator identity. Their passkeys should not share an RP ID with customers. Two options fit. A **host-only RP ID such as `ops.tetsu.example`** cannot be requested from `tetsu.example` or a sibling, because the RP ID must be a suffix of the caller's domain. A **separate registrable domain** adds cross-site isolation and its own HSTS policy. The second is cleaner, and the first costs nothing extra. Either way, operators should use hardware-bound keys, the L3 example in 6.3.3, and whether operator accounts exist at all is still [#24](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/24)'s call. Staff need a smaller choice. If the customer RP ID is the apex, staff can share it, with the overlap problem from the previous section, or use a host-only `app.tetsu.example` RP ID that keeps staff passkeys out of customer pages. Moving to an apex RP ID later does not carry narrower passkeys with it. One vendor and one note inference say widening is safe ([Corbado](https://www.corbado.com/de/blog/webauthn-relying-party-id-rpid-passkeys)), but the spec's reading is that a credential answers only to its own RP ID, so old passkeys keep working only on their original host. Okta lists a known iOS failure when the RP ID is a root domain, without details ([Okta Support](https://support.okta.com/help/s/article/passkey-registration-authentication-fails-on-ios-when-relying-party-id-in-okta-is-set-to-a-root-domain)), which a real-device test should cover.

## Frames show booking, and migrations cost once or per business

[ADR-0031](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/blob/main/docs/adr/0031-front-end-model-revised.md) already settled that customers log in at the top level, never in a frame, and the evidence backs it. WebAuthn is off by default in cross-origin frames. The business would have to paste embed code with `allow="publickey-credentials-get"` and `publickey-credentials-create`. Creating a passkey in a frame needs a user gesture and cannot be conditional ([WebAuthn L3 §5.10](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#sctn-iframe-guidance)). Browser data conflicts. MDN's compatibility data marks Safari false for both, while a vendor says Safari allows login but not creation ([MDN BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/html/elements/iframe.json); [Corbado](https://corbado.com/blog/iframe-passkeys-webauthn/enable-passkeys-iframe)). Even when the ceremony succeeds, the frame's session cookie is partitioned per embedding site, so "users must sign in again for each embedding site" ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/webauthn-within-iframe)), and the spec warns of clickjacking ([WebAuthn L3 §13.4.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#sctn-seccons-visibility)). Under option A the frame is already a Tetsu origin, so a later frame-based passkey login would at least use the same credentials. The sound pattern is a frame for browsing and booking, with a top-level window or redirect to Tetsu's login host for anything that needs an account. Passkey autofill follows the same line. It offers only passkeys whose RP ID is valid for the page, so under A it works on Tetsu hosts but not on custom domains, where a "Sign in" button starts the handoff ([WebAuthn L3 §5.1.4](https://www.w3.org/TR/webauthn-3/#sctn-getAssertion)).

No WebAuthn mechanism moves a passkey to a new RP ID. The Signal API can only hide, delete, or rename credentials within one RP ID. It ships in Chrome 132 and Safari 26, not Firefox ([MDN BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/PublicKeyCredential.json)), and a WebKit bug reports `signalAllAcceptedCredentials` never resolving ([WebKit 298951](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=298951)). A move therefore means re-enrollment: log in with the old passkey, create a new one on the new domain, and clean up the old one. X's move from twitter.com is the public example. X required passkey and security key users to re-enroll on x.com by 10 November 2025, and afterwards users reported errors, login loops, and lockouts ([Dataconomy](https://dataconomy.com/2025/11/13/passkey-migration-to-x-com-leaves-many-x-users-stuck-in-login-loops); [Security Affairs](https://securityaffairs.com/183928/security/x-warns-users-to-re-enroll-passkeys-and-yubikeys-for-2fa-by-nov-10.html)). X's passkeys were a second factor on top of a password, so users had a fallback. A passkey-first Tetsu would push affected users into email recovery all at once. The options differ in where that risk falls. Under A, Tetsu takes it once, when it chooses its own domain, and can take it before anyone enrolls. Under B, every business's domain change creates its own small version of the X event. Under C, the risk follows the label budget and the least capable browser.

| Decision 3 option | Strongest for | Weakest on | Evidence to gather before choosing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Tetsu apex RP ID, central branded login, handoff | Portability, one phishing-resistant origin, embeds, social login | Prompt names Tetsu; duplicate entries for per-business accounts | How managers show two accounts with one domain and one email; handoff test on a second domain |
| A2. Per-business Tetsu subdomain as RP ID, handoff | Per-business labels with portability | Slug renames strand passkeys; wildcard and cookie-tossing work | Render wildcard cost and certificate test |
| B. RP ID per business domain | Branding; matches business-owned accounts | Re-enrollment on every domain change; per-tenant WebAuthn config | Business demand for own-domain login |
| C. Related Origin Requests | A few Tetsu brand domains | Caps at about 5 labels; Firefox unclear | Firefox and Safari test, if ever needed |
| Operators: separate domain or host-only RP ID | Keeps the highest-value keys out of tenant reach | One more hostname | Waits on #24 |

## Conclusion

The research reframes decision 3. The urgent question is not which option to pick but when the choice becomes permanent. That happens the first time a real person enrolls, and on the demo host that would happen on a domain Tetsu does not own and cannot leave. So the cheapest action is to register a domain before #20 lets anyone else enroll, which keeps every option open. Choosing among the options themselves is a smaller step. A, A2, and B differ less in security than in who absorbs a future domain change: Tetsu once, or each business separately.

The finding most likely to reshape #9 is the clash between per-business customer accounts and a shared RP ID. Lee Park's two accounts become two near-identical passkeys under one Tetsu domain. That pushes the ADR to choose among a username label, an email-first flow, a per-business subdomain, or a Tetsu-held credential linked to business-owned accounts, and the last of these reopens the ownership question #9 started with. A short real-device test, enrolling two accounts with one email under one RP ID on iOS, Android, and a password manager, would settle more of this decision than further reading.
