# Identity and sign-in research

Research for [#9](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/9), run 2026-11-11 by Kurt Medley with a coding
agent: how Tetsu's operators, business staff, and customers sign up and sign in. Seven research passes covered
industry leaders, sign-in methods against ASVS 5.0.0, multi-tenant patterns, operator access, build versus buy,
and recovery; their notes, with every source, are in [identity/](identity/). This is research, not a decision.
Where it disagrees with an ADR, the ADR wins. Facts and prices are as read on 2026-11-11.

## Passkeys carry Tetsu's sign-in across three realms

The evidence points to one shape for Tetsu: **a global identity with per-business memberships for staff, a separate account per business for customers, and a separate, hardened realm for operators, with a user-verifying passkey as the everyday sign-in credential in all three.** A passkey is the only consumer-friendly method that meets ASVS 5.0.0 Level 2's multi-factor rule (6.3.3) in a single step. "Continue with email" and "continue with Google" do not meet it alone: email is not an authentication factor at all, and Google does not reliably assert MFA. So a Linear-like front door still works, but email and Google become the way a person *enters and recovers* an account, and a passkey (or TOTP as a fallback) becomes the way they *sign in*. Leaders converge on this realm split: Linear, Stripe, Shopify, and Square give staff one identity across workspaces or merchants, and Shopify scopes customer accounts to each store. Mindbody's 2025 move to a universal customer account shows what goes wrong otherwise. For implementation, the strongest fit is a hybrid: build passkeys, email codes, TOTP, and Google OIDC in Rust from mature crates, and buy or self-host a broker for SAML later, so Tetsu never parses SAML XML. A hosted IdP (WorkOS is cheapest at $0 to 1M MAU) is the fallback if the build is too much for one maintainer. The weakest evidence covers customer-side conversion under mandatory MFA, passkey portability when a business moves to its own domain, and several vendor details that came from prior knowledge rather than fresh sources. This report frames options for the ADR on [#9](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/9). It does not make the decision.

## Leaders give staff one identity and give each merchant its own customers

Collaboration SaaS settled on one global user with memberships long ago. Linear lets one browser hold several signed-in accounts and switch workspaces without signing in again, and signing out ends all sessions, so sessions belong to the person, not the workspace ([Linear](https://linear.app/docs/login-methods)). GitHub, Atlassian, Figma, and Notion follow the same model. Notion checks a workspace's sign-in rules "when you open a workspace" rather than at global sign-in ([Notion](https://www.notion.com/help/log-in-and-out)). Slack is the outlier: **the same email gives "separate Slack accounts for each" workspace**, glued together by one email code that finds them all ([Slack](https://slack.com/help/articles/212681477-Sign-in-to-Slack)). Commerce platforms do the same for merchant staff. **Stripe "supports distinct Dashboard roles for each Stripe account"** ([Stripe](https://docs.stripe.com/dashboard/teams/roles)), and Square links one Team login to several employers. Square's forums also show the failure mode Tetsu must avoid. Invites that push an existing user to "create an account" fail with "email already has an account" ([Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Staff-Payroll/Cannot-onboard-employee-bec-email-is-associated-to-other-Square/m-p/745805)). An invite to a known email should mean "sign in and accept".

The customer side is a spectrum, and the end Tetsu wants is well documented. Shopify's new customer accounts are configured per storefront, keyed by shop ID, and sign in with a **6-digit emailed code with no password, sessions up to 365 days, and Google, Facebook, or Shop passkeys as options** ([Shopify Help](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/customers/customer-accounts/new-customer-accounts); [shopify.dev](https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/headless/building-with-the-customer-account-api/getting-started)). That per-store scoping is inferred from the docs, not stated outright. The Shop app sits on top as an opt-in platform wallet. Mindbody went the other way: its **July 31, 2025 "Universal Account" caused sign-in failures when studio and universal profiles were not synced** ([Bay Studios](https://bay-studios.com/mindbody-universal-account-update/)). A low-authority review site says operators objected that Mindbody treats studio clients as its own, and that the rollout was partly rolled back ([Vibefam](https://vibefam.com/mindbody-review-pricing-features-pros-cons-2026/)). The rollback claim has only that one source. Identity vendors name the same split: Clerk says that when businesses "serve their own users", each needs an isolated pool where "a user must create a separate account for each organization" ([Clerk](https://clerk.com/docs/guides/multi-tenant-architecture)). AWS warns that pool-per-tenant has a "high" level of effort when every pool is a deployed resource ([AWS Cognito](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/cognito/latest/developerguide/bp_user-pool-based-multi-tenancy.html)). In one Postgres schema that cost mostly disappears, because the "pool" is just `UNIQUE (tenant_id, lower(email))`.

Guardians and walk-ins have clear prior art. Glofox child accounts "will not be able to log in themselves, but the guardian can purchase memberships and book on behalf of the child", with a studio-set minimum age ([Glofox](https://support.glofox.com/hc/en-us/articles/46478887830164-How-to-Set-up-Family-Accounts)). Booksy tags each booking with a relationship and sends reminders to both booker and attendee ([Booksy](https://biz.booksy.com/en-us/blog/family-friends-let-clients-schedule-appointments-for-loved-ones)). Patient portals add an age threshold: teens get their own login from 12 or 13, and at 18 the adult confirms their own email ([Brown Health](https://www.brownhealth.org/node/289136); [UT Physicians](https://www.utphysicians.com/adult-proxy/)). This points to a model that separates the *account that signs in* from the *client profile that receives the service*. A walk-in is a profile that staff create with no account. A guardian is an account linked to a minor's profile. A family sharing one email is one account with several profiles. Coming of age creates a new account and grants it `self` on the existing profile. This matters for MFA: the guardian account in the reference businesses holds a 15-year-old's waiver, which is why [ADR-0029](../adr/0029-security-target-revised.md) already rejected relaxing MFA for customers.

## Only a user-verifying passkey meets Level 2 multi-factor in one step

ASVS 5.0.0 6.3.3 (L2) requires "either a multi-factor authentication mechanism or a combination of single-factor authentication mechanisms... in order to access the application". That covers every account, not just privileged ones. Relaxing it requires "a fully documented rationale and a comprehensive set of mitigating controls" ([ASVS V6](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/v5.0.0_release/5.0/en/0x15-V6-Authentication.md)). ASVS defines factors in NIST terms, and **NIST says "Email SHALL NOT be used for out-of-band authentication"**, while allowing email confirmation codes and issued recovery codes because they are "not authentication" ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)). ASVS's V6.6 intro likewise says "e-mail and VOIP are not permitted" as out-of-band channels. NIST's syncable-authenticator appendix makes synced passkeys AAL2-capable *if* the verifier requires user verification: "If the user is not verified, agencies SHALL treat the authenticator as a single-factor cryptographic authenticator" ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)). ASVS never says in its own words that a passkey meets 6.3.3. That conclusion follows only because ASVS defers to NIST, so the ADR should state the reasoning.

| Method | Factor status | Meets 6.3.3 alone? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Passkey, UV required (synced or device-bound) | Multi-factor cryptographic | **Yes** | Phishing resistant. Synced passkeys are not L3; device-bound keys meet ASVS's L3 example |
| Passkey without UV | Have only | No | Must set `userVerification: "required"` or downgrade |
| Password (Argon2id) | Know | No | Needs a second factor; 6.2.x rules and breached-password check apply |
| TOTP | Have | No; valid second factor | Phishable; NIST AAL2 also requires offering a phishing-resistant option |
| Recovery (lookup) codes | Have | No; second factor or recovery | Single use, hashed if under 112 bits (6.5.1, 6.5.2) |
| SMS OTP | Have, restricted | No; second factor only under 6.6.1 | Requires a stronger alternative and a risk notice; banned at L3 |
| Email code or magic link | **Not a factor** | No, not even as a second factor (strictly) | Fine for email verification and as one recovery code |
| Google / Apple / Microsoft OIDC | Depends on IdP assertion | Only if `amr`/`acr` shows MFA | 6.8.4 requires assuming single-factor otherwise |
| Enterprise SAML / OIDC SSO | Depends on IdP | Same, via AuthnContext or `amr` | A contract saying "tenant IdP enforces MFA" is a documented relaxation, not proof |

Sources: [ASVS V6](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/v5.0.0_release/5.0/en/0x15-V6-Authentication.md); [NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html).

One note disagrees with this strict reading. It argues that 6.3.3's "combination of single-factor mechanisms" could admit password plus email link at L2, because the explicit email ban (6.3.6) is L3 only. That reading clashes with the V6.6 intro and with NIST, and it would not survive an L3 upgrade. The ADR should treat it as a relaxation, not a compliant path.

Google is the critical case for "continue with Google". **Google's `amr` claim "may not be present even when requested"**, though when present a `mfa` value "confirms the authentication event met Google's requirements for Multi-Factor Authentication" ([Google](https://developers.google.com/identity/siwg/security-bundle)). Auth0 treats a missing `amr` as not MFA ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/docs/secure/multi-factor-authentication/step-up-authentication/configure-step-up-authentication-for-web-apps.md)). The safe design trusts Google's MFA only when `amr` contains `mfa` with a fresh `auth_time`. Otherwise it asks for a local passkey or TOTP after the Google step. Apple and Microsoft `amr` behavior was not verified. The UX data favors passkey-first despite its caveats. FIDO's October 2026 index of 15 large consumer services reports **93% sign-in success for passkeys versus 78% for passwords plus traditional MFA**, 2.8× faster sign-ins, and a participant estimate that showing passkeys first "would roughly double its passkey sign-in share" ([FIDO Passkey Index](https://fidoalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Passkey-Index-October-2026.pdf)). Those figures are self-reported among enrolled users, so they say little about how many booking customers will enroll.

The resulting Linear-like flow looks like this. The front door offers "Continue with passkey", "Continue with Google", and "Continue with email". At sign-up, email or Google proves the contact address, and the next screen enrolls a passkey (TOTP as a fallback) before the account can reach anything. Later sign-ins are a single passkey tap. Email-code sign-in followed by a passkey or TOTP is the path for a new device, and SAML sits on top for businesses that need it. Codes beat magic links on both mail scanners and cross-device use. Scanners such as Safe Links can consume one-time links, and a code can be read on a phone and typed on a desktop ([Stytch](https://stytch.com/docs/b2b/guides/magic-links/overview); [Supabase](https://github.com/orgs/supabase/discussions/41618)). That evidence is vendor and forum material. Slack and Notion use codes, and Linear accepts either. One conflict is already partly settled. The WebAuthn API is JavaScript-only, and [ADR-0031](../adr/0031-front-end-model-revised.md) already lets pages use script for "steps that cannot [work without it], such as... a passkey". So passkeys need only a small same-origin script under `script-src 'self'`, not a change of front-end model.

## Server-side bindings, not cookies, keep tenants apart

On one host with `/<slug>` paths, the cookie cannot isolate tenants. ASVS 3.3.3 (L2) requires the `__Host-` prefix, which forces `Path=/` and no `Domain`. Cookie Path is not a security boundary anyway ([ASVS V3](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/v5.0.0_release/5.0/en/0x12-V3-Web-Frontend-Security.md); [RFC 6265 §8.5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6265#section-8.5), not re-fetched). Isolation therefore has to live in the server-side session row: an opaque token of at least 128 bits (7.2.3) keys a row holding `realm`, `subject_id`, `tenant_id` (required for customers), `auth_time`, and the methods used. Every request compares the URL's tenant, or the Host header once custom domains exist, against that binding, and **fails closed rather than silently switching**. This is how ASVS 8.4.1, the only multi-tenant requirement, applies to identity ([ASVS V8](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/v5.0.0_release/5.0/en/0x17-V8-Authorization.md)). Three confusions to test for: a tenant A customer session accepted at `/tenant-b`, a login or reset token minted for A redeemed at B, and a staff role cached from A applied at B. Every token should carry and check `tenant_id` and `realm`. The session should rotate on sign-in, step-up, and change of active business (7.2.4). The Postgres side adds composite foreign keys `(tenant_id, customer_id)`, so a booking cannot reference another tenant's customer even through an app bug. Row-level security helps only as a second layer: owners and BYPASSRLS roles skip it silently, and tenant context must use `SET LOCAL` so it never leaks across pooled connections ([Bytebase](https://www.bytebase.com/blog/postgres-row-level-security-footguns/)). Staff sign-in lookups happen before tenant context exists, so they belong in one narrow, separately reviewed function.

Per-business SSO and MFA policy should follow GitHub's two-layer model: a global github.com session plus "an active SAML session" per SSO organization ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/authenticating-with-saml-single-sign-on/about-authentication-with-saml-single-sign-on)). For Tetsu, a business that requires SSO checks it when a staff member *enters* that business, so a person who works at two salons is never locked out of the second by the first's policy. Linear and Calendly also exempt owners from enforced methods to prevent lockout ([Calendly](https://help.calendly.com/hc/en-us/articles/360056867653)).

Custom domains are where the evidence is thinnest and the design is costliest to retrofit. Cookies cannot cross registrable domains, so each custom domain needs its own session. Two patterns exist. In the first, each domain runs its own sign-in and sets its own `__Host-` cookie, which fits per-business customers who never need cross-business sign-in. In the second, a central auth host returns a single-use code bound to the target host, which the custom domain exchanges server-side. Substack does this, per a third-party developer's description ([substack-mcp PR](https://github.com/conorbronsdon/substack-mcp/pull/166)), and Clerk calls it "satellite domains" ([Clerk changelog](https://clerk.com/changelog/2023-09-01)). Passkeys raise the stakes, because **a passkey is bound to the domain where it was created**. A customer passkey enrolled on a Tetsu hostname will not work on `book.salon.com` unless Tetsu keeps sign-in on its own host, or relies on WebAuthn's Related Origin Requests. The latter is prior knowledge, and browser support was not verified. This choice links #9 to the hostname decision in [#8](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/8). The options are to always sign customers in on a Tetsu-owned host with a branded page (portable passkeys, weaker branding), to sign in on the business's domain (strong branding, re-enrollment if the domain changes), or to adopt Related Origin Requests after a real browser test. Social login on custom domains also needs a central callback host with signed `state`, because IdPs limit dynamic redirect URIs. That is an inference, not sourced.

Account linking has a well-documented failure record. **Pre-hijacking research found at least 35 of 75 popular services vulnerable** ([USENIX Security 2022](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/sudhodanan)). nOAuth showed that attackers can set Entra `email` claims, and Microsoft now says to key on `tid` plus `oid` ([Descope](https://descope.com/blog/post/noauth); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/migrate-off-email-claim-authorization)). Truffle Security showed that buying a defunct startup's domain let the buyer recreate ex-employees' Google accounts and sign in to Slack, Notion, and HR tools that keyed on email or `hd` ([Truffle Security](https://trufflesecurity.com/blog/millions-at-risk-due-to-google-s-oauth-flaw)). The rules that follow are ASVS 6.8.1 and 10.5.2: key federated identities on `(issuer, sub)` and treat email as a contact attribute. Link a new method only while the person is signed in to the existing account, or after fresh proof through its factor. Never auto-merge on `email_verified`. Make no account usable until its email is verified, and expire every session and token created before verification. Domain auto-join, which Linear, Figma, and WorkOS offer, should stay off for small businesses on shared or lapsing domains.

## Operators need no standing access and a separate realm

Every support-tool incident in the notes was an attack on operator identity, not on tenant-isolation logic. Twitter's 2020 attackers phoned staff posing as IT, and over 1,000 employees had access to the internal tools ([Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/threat-intelligence/twitter-hack-analysis-drives-calls-for-greater-security-regulation/d/d-id/1339193)). Mailchimp's support console was entered with phished credentials, reaching 133 accounts in January 2023 ([The Record](https://therecord.media/more-than-100-mailchimp-accounts-accessed-via-social-engineering-cyberattack/)). Retool's attacker used the internal support instance to change emails and reset passwords for 27 customers, after Google Authenticator's cloud sync turned MFA into "single-factor-authentication" ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/mfa-isnt-mfa)). Okta's 2023 support breach began with a service account saved to a personal Chrome profile. It took HAR files from 134 customers and hijacked five customers' sessions, and customers detected it before Okta did ([Okta RCA](https://sec.okta.com/articles/2023/11/unauthorized-access-oktas-support-case-management-system-root-cause)).

The reference designs agree on "no standing access". Microsoft Customer Lockbox requires a ticket-linked request and a second internal approver, then the customer's own approver. **Unapproved requests expire after 12 hours, and approved access lasts at most 4 hours through "pre-approved cmdlets"**, logged as "Microsoft Operator" in the customer's audit log. Its notification emails carry no sign-in links ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/purview/customer-lockbox-requests)). Google Access Approval adds an auto-approved break-glass path for outages, logged distinctly ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/assured-workloads/access-approval/docs/overview)). Stripe is the lighter SaaS version: an access-request link the merchant approves, an editable expiry, removal at any time, and an entry in Security history ([Stripe](https://support.stripe.com/questions/grant-stripe-employees-access-to-your-dashboard)). Zendesk's long pre-grants ("indefinitely") are flagged by Datadog as a risk ([Datadog](https://docs.datadoghq.com/security/default_rules/zendesk-account-assumption-settings)).

For Tetsu, the options run from simplest to strongest. The simplest is no operator access to tenant data at all, with support working from what the business shares. Next is a support grant object holding ticket, scope, duration in hours, business approver, automatic expiry, and revocation, read-only by default. The strongest adds Lockbox-style internal two-person approval and a logged break-glass path. All three share some baselines: operators sign in only with phishing-resistant passkeys, ideally hardware-bound (the L3 bar of 6.3.3, which NYDFS's Twitter findings support). They use a separate realm and hostname (3.5.4). Impersonation runs under the operator's own identity, checked against the grant (8.3.3, 6.3.4), and **operators can never change a business's sign-in email or credentials** (the Retool and Mailchimp takeover primitive, and 6.4.6). Every operator read and action goes to an append-only, customer-visible log shipped off-host (V16, tied to [#27](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/27)). Whether operator accounts exist at all is [#24](https://github.com/heavymedl-labs/tetsu/issues/24)'s call, so #9 should define the realm and leave its existence to #24.

## Build the core in Rust, broker SAML, and keep a hosted fallback

Requiring MFA for every account rules out the free tiers of Clerk and Auth0, since **Clerk's Hobby plan excludes MFA and passkeys** and Auth0 Free excludes Pro MFA ([Clerk](https://clerk.com/pricing); [Auth0](https://auth0.com/pricing)).

| Option | Cost at 1k / 10k / 100k MAU | SAML | Fit with server-rendered, no-JS pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| WorkOS AuthKit | $0 / $0 / $0 (free to 1M MAU); +$99/mo custom domain | $125/connection (1 to 15) | Hosted redirect plus server code exchange; good fit |
| Clerk Pro | ~$25 / ~$25 / ~$1,025/mo, before B2B add-ons | 1 included, then $75 each | JS components and a session refreshed every minute; poor fit |
| Auth0 B2B Essentials | $300 / $2,100 / sales contract | 3 included, then $100 | Universal Login redirect; expensive |
| Stytch (Twilio since Nov 2025) | Free to 10k; unpublished above | 5 free, then $125 | Not assessed |
| AWS Cognito Essentials | ~$0 / $0 / ~$1,350; free tier figures conflict | $0.015 per federated MAU | Hosted pages; AWS-specific |
| Self-host (Keycloak, Zitadel, Ory) | Infra plus ops time | Keycloak yes; Ory via Polis | A second stateful service on Render |
| Build in Rust | Maintainer time; ~2 to 4k lines plus tests (estimate) | Via a broker | Native fit |

Sources: [WorkOS](https://workos.com/pricing); [Clerk](https://clerk.com/pricing); [Auth0](https://auth0.com/pricing); [Stytch](https://stytch.com/pricing); [Twilio 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1447669/000144766926000021/twlo-20251231.htm); [AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/cognito/pricing).

Hosted providers move the risk to the vendor's operations. Clerk's February 2025 bad update took about 3,700 customers down for 26 minutes. Its token model, refreshed every minute, creates what its own postmortem calls a "strong uptime dependency", and it has posted several more outage postmortems through March 2026 ([Clerk](https://clerk.com/blog/postmortem-feb-6-2025-service-outage)). A redirect-based IdP behind Tetsu's own session cookie limits an outage to new sign-ins. Lock-in comes from user IDs and passkeys. Passkeys bind to the relying party's domain, so **a vendor-hosted sign-in domain strands passkeys there** unless Tetsu pays for a custom domain. Self-hosting doubles the operations surface. Licenses are also drifting: Zitadel moved to AGPL in v3 ([Zitadel](https://zitadel.com/blog/apache-to-agpl)). The Rust IdPs, Rauthy and Kanidm, are single-organization servers, not multi-tenant customer identity platforms ([Rauthy](https://github.com/sebadob/rauthy)).

Building fits Tetsu's stack and ADR-0003's "no provider-specific services". webauthn-rs anchors it, and its README claims a SUSE security audit, though no public report was found. Its one 2026 advisory was low severity and applied only to an off-by-default option ([docs.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/webauthn-rs/0.4.5/source/README.md); [GHSA-22w3-693w-x895](https://advisories.gitlab.com/cargo/webauthn-rs-core/GHSA-22w3-693w-x895/)). The crates `openidconnect`, `argon2`, and `totp-rs` cover the rest, from prior knowledge. Versions still need checking under [ADR-0010](../adr/0010-evaluating-technology.md). SAML is the exception. The only Rust crate, samael, is pre-1.0 and wraps the C library xmlsec1 ([samael](https://docs.rs/crate/samael/0.0.20/source/README.md)), and mature libraries keep failing: ruby-saml had a CVSS 9.8 parser-differential bypass in March 2025 and an incomplete fix rated 9.1 later that year ([NVD](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2025-25291); [Averlon](https://research.averlon.ai/vulnerability-intelligence/cve/CVE-2025-66567)). The hybrid therefore puts SAML behind a broker that presents each customer's IdP to Tetsu as plain OIDC. WorkOS SSO costs $0 until the first SAML customer. Ory Polis, formerly SAML Jackson and Apache 2.0, avoids per-connection fees but adds a service ([Ory](https://www.ory.com/blog/introducing-ory-polis-for-enterprise-single-sign-on)). Whether WorkOS SSO alone speaks standard OIDC to `openidconnect` was not confirmed. If the build proves too heavy, the fallback is WorkOS AuthKit for staff and operators, with customer accounts built locally, since per-business pools are Tetsu's own data model either way.

## Recovery, not sign-in, is where accounts actually fall

ASVS 6.4.3 (L2) forbids a reset that "bypass[es] any enabled multi-factor authentication mechanisms", and 6.4.4 requires identity evidence for a lost factor "at the same level as during enrollment" ([ASVS V6](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/v5.0.0/5.0/en/0x15-V6-Authentication.md)). ASVS never defines that level for accounts with no identity proofing, so the ADR must interpret it. NIST 800-63B-4 supplies a concrete AAL2 recipe: **two recovery codes obtained through different methods, or one code plus a remaining bound authenticator**. Saved codes need at least 64 bits, issued email codes last at most 24 hours, and "account recovery SHALL cause a notification" ([NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html)). The recovery incidents are the starkest in the notes. In MGM's 2023 breach, a help-desk call reportedly led to an MFA reset on a super-admin account ([Kolide](https://www.kolide.com/blog/what-everyone-got-wrong-about-the-mgm-hack)). CISA's Scattered Spider advisory describes getting staff to "transfer the employee's MFA to a device they control" ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/news/366559974/CISA-FBI-issue-alert-for-ongoing-Scattered-Spider-activity)). Bribed support agents cost Coinbase an estimated $180M to $400M ([eSecurity Planet](https://www.esecurityplanet.com/news/coinbase-rejects-ransom-data-leak/)).

Recovery can be tiered by realm. Staff recover through the business owner, who *starts* a factor reset but never sees or sets the secret (the 6.4.6 pattern). The staff member then enrolls a new factor through a short-lived link to their verified email, and every address gets a notice. Owners and operators, as accounts of last resort, need a slow, notified path with a second approver. Customers lean on synced passkeys, which rarely vanish with a device, plus recovery codes offered at enrollment. For those with neither, the option is delayed recovery: an emailed code, a wait of days, notices to every address, and automatic cancellation if the account signs in normally. Apple's model is the precedent, though its details come from secondary sources ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/16/google_gmail_trusted_contacts/)). With only email as a channel, this is single-channel recovery, so it must be recorded as a documented compensating control under 6.3.3. Support should have no tool that rebinds a factor without the account holder acting.

Enforcing MFA from the first sign-up avoids GitHub's and Roku's retrofits. **GitHub's staged mandate reached nearly 95% opt-in, cut 2FA support tickets by a third, and a 28-day checkup "helped 25% of users successfully reconfigure"** ([GitHub](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/securing-millions-of-developers-through-2fa/)). Roku turned on 2FA for all accounts only after credential stuffing hit 576,000 accounts ([SC Media](https://scmagazine.com/news/roku-activates-2fa-for-80m-users-after-breach-of-576k-accounts)). Snowflake shows the platform blame when MFA is tenant opt-in: Mandiant "assesses MFA would have prevented compromise" ([Dark Reading](https://darkreading.com/cloud-security/snowflake-cloud-accounts-rampant-credential-issues)). The same lessons apply to Tetsu's own setup. Microsoft's Midnight Blizzard breach started at a test tenant with no MFA, so demo and seed accounts need MFA too, or no sign-in at all ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/microsoft-reveals-how-hackers-breached-its-exchange-online-accounts/amp/)). The Authy endpoint leaked 33.4M phone numbers through enumeration, which argues for adopting 6.3.8's enumeration resistance at L2 even though it is an L3 item ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2024/07/authy-phone-numbers-accessed-by-cybercriminals-warns-twilio)). Passwordless design sidesteps 6.2.x and breached-password checks entirely. If passwords are offered, HIBP's range API meets 6.2.12 by sending only a 5-character hash prefix ([HIBP](https://haveibeenpwned.com/API/v3)). Skipping SMS removes SIM swaps, SMS pumping, and 6.6.1's conditions. Code and recovery mail needs SPF, DKIM, and aligned DMARC on a dedicated transactional subdomain. The mail provider choice is unresearched and needs its own ADR-0010 evaluation.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the usual framing of "Linear-like sign-in". Under ASVS Level 2, the familiar buttons survive, but they are doors, not locks. Google and email establish who someone is and get them back in, and the passkey does the authenticating. Given that, the expensive decisions for #9 are not the sign-in methods. They are the ones that are hard to retrofit: the relying-party domain that every passkey will be bound to, the `(issuer, sub)` key and link-only-while-signed-in rule, the account-versus-client-profile split that makes guardians and walk-ins natural, and the session row that binds realm and tenant on every request. Each of these is cheap now and costly once real customers have enrolled.

The largest unknown is whether booking customers will accept enrolling a passkey to book a haircut. No source measures conversion under mandatory consumer MFA in booking or retail, and FIDO's success rates describe users who already enrolled. That makes a customer-side relaxation the most likely future pressure on this ADR. It also argues for recording the evidence that would justify a relaxation (enrollment and drop-off rates from the walking skeleton) before any real customer data arrives, rather than guessing now. The second unknown is the custom-domain passkey path, which only a real browser test, coordinated with #8, can settle.
